### Part 0: Why is Fractional Saturation Depth Not Linear with Laser Power?

In saturated absorption spectroscopy, the pump beam bleaches the resonant velocity class ($v_z \approx 0$). As a function of the saturation parameter $s_0 = I_{\text{pump}} / I_{\text{sat}} = P / P_{\text{sat}}$, the absorption coefficient of that velocity class drops from its unsaturated value $\alpha_0$ to:
$$\alpha_{\text{sat}}(P) = \frac{\alpha_0}{\sqrt{1 + P / P_{\text{sat}}}} \quad \left(\text{or } \frac{\alpha_0}{1 + P / P_{\text{sat}}} \text{ for a single homogeneous packet}\right)$$
Therefore, the fractional saturation depth—the height of the Lamb dip $\Delta\alpha = \alpha_0 - \alpha_{\text{sat}}$ divided by the unpumped Doppler absorption $\alpha_0$—is:
$$\frac{\Delta V_{\text{peak}}}{\Delta V_{\text{Doppler}}} \propto 1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + P / P_{\text{sat}}}}$$

* **At very low power ($P \ll P_{\text{sat}}$):** A Taylor expansion gives $\frac{\Delta\alpha}{\alpha_0} \approx \frac{1}{2}\frac{P}{P_{\text{sat}}}$, which is **linear in $P$**.
* **At moderate-to-high power ($P \gtrsim P_{\text{sat}}$):** Once the pump has already bleached a significant fraction of the atoms in the $v_z = 0$ velocity class, doubling the power cannot bleach more than $100\%$ of the available atoms—there are fewer ground-state atoms left to excite. Thus the fractional depth **bends over sublinearly (saturates)**.
  * You can see this directly in your Session 10 data: increasing power by $8.7\times$ (from $99\,\mu\text{W}$ to $859\,\mu\text{W}$) only increases the $^{87}\text{Rb}$ cycling peak fractional depth by $4.2\times$ ($3.2\% \to 13.4\%$) and the $CO(2,3)$ crossover depth by $3.0\times$ ($15.0\% \to 44.5\%$, because crossovers have a lower effective saturation power $P_{\text{sat}}$ and saturate even earlier).

### Part 2: Why Coherent Dark States Cannot Form on the Cycling Transitions ($F \to F'=F+1$)

A **dark state** $|\psi_{\text{dark}}\rangle = \sum_m c_m |F, m\rangle$ is a ground-state superposition that cannot absorb light:
$$\hat{V}|\psi_{\text{dark}}\rangle = -(\hat{\vec{d}}\cdot\vec{E})|\psi_{\text{dark}}\rangle = 0$$

Whether a dark state exists is a coordinate-independent property of the operator $\hat{V}$ (changing basis by a unitary rotation cannot create or destroy a zero eigenvalue). To check if a linearly polarized beam $\vec{E}$ has a dark state on $F \to F'$, temporarily inspect the basis quantized along $\vec{E}$ itself, where $\vec{E}$ drives purely $\Delta m_E = 0$ transitions $|F, m_E\rangle \to |F', m_E\rangle$:
$$\hat{V}|\psi\rangle = \sum_{m_E=-F}^{F} c_{m_E} \langle F, m_E; 1, 0 | F', m_E \rangle |F', m_E\rangle$$
Since the excited states $|F', m_E\rangle$ are mutually orthogonal, $\hat{V}|\psi\rangle = 0$ requires $c_{m_E}\,\langle F, m_E; 1, 0 | F', m_E \rangle = 0$ for every $m_E$:
* **If $F' = F - 1$:** There are $2F+1$ ground states but only $2F-1$ excited states; $m_E = \pm F$ have no excited targets ($\text{CG} = 0$), creating **2 dark states**.
* **If $F' = F$:** $\langle F, 0; 1, 0 | F, 0 \rangle = 0$, so $m_E = 0$ is **1 dark state** (which looks like a coherent superposition $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(|+1\rangle + |-1\rangle)$ if written in a perpendicular basis).
* **If $F' = F + 1$ (your cycling transitions $^{87}\text{Rb } F=2 \to F'=3$ and $^{85}\text{Rb } F=3 \to F'=4$):** There are $2F+3$ excited states—more than the $2F+1$ ground states—and the Clebsch-Gordan coefficient is:
  $$\langle F, m_E; 1, 0 | F+1, m_E \rangle = \sqrt{\frac{(F+1)^2 - m_E^2}{(F+1)(2F+1)}} > 0 \quad \text{for all } m_E \in \{-F, \dots, +F\}$$
  Because $(F+1)^2 - m_E^2 \ge 2F+1 > 0$, **none of the coefficients is zero**. Thus $c_{m_E} = 0$ for all $m_E$: **no dark state (coherent or incoherent) exists on a cycling transition.**

### Part 3: How Zeeman Alignment is Formed and Why Larmor Precession Only Washes Out Its Off-Diagonal Components

Now return to our standard **$\hat{b} = \vec{B}/|\vec{B}|$ basis** ($|F, m_B\rangle$).

Even without dark states, a linearly polarized pump beam tilted at angle $\alpha_{\text{pump}}$ to $\vec{B}$ excites different $m_B$ states at different rates and creates a ground-state density matrix $\hat{\rho}_g$ with elements $\rho_{m_B, m'_B} = \langle F, m_B | \hat{\rho}_g | F, m'_B \rangle$.

In the $\hat{b}$-basis, the magnetic Hamiltonian is diagonal: $\hat{H}_B |F, m_B\rangle = \hbar\Omega_L m_B |F, m_B\rangle$, where $\Omega_L = g_F \mu_B |\vec{B}|/\hbar$ is the Larmor frequency. The Liouville–von Neumann equation for each matrix element $\rho_{m_B, m'_B}$ is:
$$\frac{d}{dt}\rho_{m_B, m'_B} = \underbrace{-i(m_B - m'_B)\Omega_L\,\rho_{m_B, m'_B}}_{\text{Larmor precession}} \;-\; \underbrace{\gamma_{\text{tr}}\big(\rho_{m_B, m'_B} - \rho_{m_B, m'_B}^{\text{eq}}\big)}_{\text{Transit relaxation}} \;+\; \underbrace{R_{m_B, m'_B}^{\text{pump}}}_{\text{Optical pumping}}$$
Setting $d\rho/dt = 0$ in steady state gives:
$$\rho_{m_B, m'_B} - \rho_{m_B, m'_B}^{\text{eq}} = \frac{R_{m_B, m'_B}^{\text{pump}}}{\gamma_{\text{tr}} + i(m_B - m'_B)\Omega_L}$$

Look at how the two types of matrix elements behave when $\Omega_L \gg \gamma_{\text{tr}}$ (in your lab, even at the minimum field $|\vec{B}| \approx 0.45\text{ G}$, $\Omega_L/2\pi \approx 315\text{ kHz}$ is $10\times$ larger than the transit rate $\gamma_{\text{tr}}/2\pi \approx 30\text{ kHz}$):

1. **Off-Diagonal Coherences in the $\vec{B}$-Basis ($m_B \ne m'_B$, i.e. Transverse Alignment):**
   * Because $m_B - m'_B = \pm 1, \pm 2 \ne 0$, the denominator is dominated by $i(m_B - m'_B)\Omega_L$:
     $$|\rho_{m_B, m'_B}| \approx \frac{|R_{m_B, m'_B}^{\text{pump}}|}{|m_B - m'_B|\,\Omega_L} \sim \mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\gamma_{\text{tr}}}{\Omega_L}\right) \to 0$$
   * **Physically:** Any coherence between different $m_B$ states represents an anisotropy *perpendicular* to $\vec{B}$ (an azimuthal $\phi$-dependence around $\vec{B}$). As atoms precess several full turns around $\vec{B}$ during their transit across the beam, this transverse coherence dephases on the fast timescale $\Omega_L^{-1}$ and is **washed out**.

2. **Diagonal Populations in the $\vec{B}$-Basis ($m_B = m'_B$, i.e. Longitudinal Alignment $n_g(m_B) \equiv \rho_{m_B, m_B}$):**
   * When $m_B = m'_B$, the factor $(m_B - m'_B)\Omega_L$ is **identically zero**:
     $$\Delta n_g(m_B) \equiv \rho_{m_B, m_B} - \frac{1}{2F+1} = \frac{R_{m_B, m_B}^{\text{pump}}}{\gamma_{\text{tr}}}$$
   * **Physically:** The states $|F, m_B\rangle$ are the energy eigenstates of $\hat{H}_B$ ($[\hat{H}_B, |F, m_B\rangle\langle F, m_B|] = 0$). **Larmor precession around $\vec{B}$ cannot change an atom's projection $m_B$ along $\vec{B}$!**
   * Therefore, any population imbalance $\Delta n_g(m_B)$ created along the $\vec{B}$-axis (e.g., more atoms in $m_B = 0$ than in $m_B = \pm 2$, or vice versa) is completely unaffected by Larmor precession and **lives for the full transit lifetime $\tau_{\text{tr}} = \gamma_{\text{tr}}^{-1} \approx 5\text{--}8\,\mu\text{s}$**.

> **Geometric Summary:** If the pump creates an ellipsoid of aligned atoms tilted at angle $\alpha_{\text{pump}}$ to $\vec{B}$, Larmor precession spins that ellipsoid rapidly around $\vec{B}$. The spinning washes out all asymmetry *perpendicular* to $\vec{B}$ in time $\Omega_L^{-1}$ (killing $\rho_{m_B, m'_B}$ for $m_B \ne m'_B$), leaving behind an axisymmetric ellipsoid (cigar or pancake) **aligned along $\vec{B}$** (the diagonal populations $n_g(m_B)$), which persists for the full transit time $\gamma_{\text{tr}}^{-1}$.

### 1. Why does $\pi$ coupling funnel population *into* $m_B = 0$ if it excites *out of* $m_B = 0$ fastest?

Your intuition is completely reasonable: if a $\pi$-polarized pump ($\Delta m_B = 0$) excites $m_B = 0$ fastest ($C_\pi(0) = 1.80$) and $m_B = \pm 2$ slowest ($C_\pi(\pm 2) = 1.00$), shouldn't atoms get evacuated from $m_B = 0$ and pile up in $m_B = \pm 2$?

On $F \to F-1$ or $F \to F$, they do! **The reason the exact opposite happens on $F \to F+1$ ($F=2 \to F'=3$) is the asymmetry of spontaneous emission from a wider excited manifold ($2F'+1 = 7$ states) back into a narrower ground manifold ($2F+1 = 5$ states).**

Follow a single scattering cycle in the $\vec{B}$-basis:
1. Suppose an atom starts in the outer ground state $|F=2, m_B = +2\rangle$ and absorbs a $\pi$ photon ($\Delta m_B = 0$) to reach $|F'=3, m'_B = +2\rangle$.
2. Now it spontaneously decays back to $F=2$ by emitting a $\pi$ ($\Delta m_B = 0$), $\sigma^+$ ($\Delta m_B = -1$), or $\sigma^-$ ($\Delta m_B = +1$) photon:
   * Can it decay **outward** to $m_B = +3$? **No—the ground state $F=2$ has no $m_B = +3$ sublevel!**
   * It can **only** decay back to $m_B = +2$ (branching fraction $B(+2 \leftarrow +2) = \frac{1}{3} = 33.3\%$) or **inward** to $m_B = +1$ (branching fraction $B(+1 \leftarrow +2) = \frac{2}{3} = 66.7\%$).
3. Conversely, if an atom is in $|F=2, m_B = +1\rangle$ and gets $\pi$-excited to $|F'=3, m'_B = +1\rangle$, its Clebsch-Gordan branching fractions to decay to $m_B = \{0, +1, +2\}$ are:
   * **Inward** to $m_B = 0$: $B(0 \leftarrow +1) = \frac{6}{15} = \mathbf{40.0\%}$
   * Same level $m_B = +1$: $B(+1 \leftarrow +1) = \frac{8}{15} = 53.3\%$
   * **Outward** to $m_B = +2$: $B(+2 \leftarrow +1) = \frac{1}{15} = \mathbf{6.7\%}$

Now write the steady-state detailed balance between adjacent ground sublevels (rate jumping outward $=$ rate jumping inward):
* **Between $m_B = +1$ and $m_B = +2$:**
  $$\begin{aligned}
  \text{Rate}(+1 \to +2) &= n_g(+1)\,\underbrace{C_\pi(+1)}_{1.60}\,\underbrace{B(+2 \leftarrow +1)}_{1/15 \, (6.7\%)} = 0.1067\,n_g(+1) \\
  \text{Rate}(+2 \to +1) &= n_g(+2)\,\underbrace{C_\pi(+2)}_{1.00}\,\underbrace{B(+1 \leftarrow +2)}_{2/3 \, (66.7\%)} = 0.6667\,n_g(+2)
  \end{aligned}$$
  Setting them equal gives:
  $$\frac{n_g(+1)}{n_g(+2)} = \frac{0.6667}{0.1067} = \mathbf{6.25}$$
  Even though $C_\pi(+1)$ is $1.6\times$ larger than $C_\pi(+2)$, the inward spontaneous decay branching ($66.7\%$) is **$10\times$ larger** than the outward decay branching ($6.7\%$)—so inward spontaneous emission wins by $10 / 1.6 = 6.25$ to $1$!
* **Between $m_B = 0$ and $m_B = +1$:**
  $$\frac{n_g(0)}{n_g(+1)} = \frac{C_\pi(+1)\,B(0 \leftarrow +1)}{C_\pi(0)\,B(+1 \leftarrow 0)} = \frac{1.60 \times 0.40}{1.80 \times 0.20} = \frac{0.64}{0.36} = \mathbf{1.78}$$
  Again, the $2:1$ inward decay bias ($0.40$ vs. $0.20$) beats the $1.125:1$ excitation ratio ($1.80$ vs. $1.60$), giving a net $11.1 : 6.25 : 1$ pile-up in $m_B = 0$ relative to $m_B = \pm 2$.

### 2. Why is the Zeeman alignment profile *broad* in frequency if its lifetime $\tau_{\text{tr}}$ is *long*?

This is a classic distinction in atomic physics between **coherence lifetime broadening** ($\Delta\nu \sim 1/2\pi\tau$, where a long lifetime makes a resonance *narrow*) and **saturation/power broadening of a population hole** ($\Delta\nu = \Gamma_0\sqrt{1 + R_0\tau}$, where a long relaxation time makes a bleached feature *broad*).

Here is the exact algebra showing why the alignment profile is broad:
* **Step 1 (The optical absorption profile still has width $\Gamma_0 = 6.07\text{ MHz}$):**
  To optically pump an atom, the laser must first excite the optical transition $5S_{1/2} \to 5P_{3/2}$. Because the $5P_{3/2}$ excited state decays in $\tau_{\text{spont}} = 26.2\text{ ns}$, the single-photon optical pumping rate as a function of laser detuning $\Delta\nu$ is a Lorentzian of width $\Gamma_0 = 6.07\text{ MHz}$:
  $$R_{\text{pump}}(\Delta\nu) = \frac{R_0}{1 + 4(\Delta\nu / \Gamma_0)^2}$$
  *(The long ground-state transit time $\tau_{\text{tr}}$ does **not** replace $\Gamma_0$ in the optical absorption cross-section!)*
* **Step 2 (Steady-state balance between optical pumping and transit relaxation):**
  At detuning $\Delta\nu$, the ground-state alignment $\Delta n_g(\Delta\nu)$ builds up at rate $R_{\text{pump}}(\Delta\nu)$ and decays at the transit rate $\gamma_{\text{tr}} = 1/\tau_{\text{tr}}$:
  $$\frac{d}{dt}\Delta n_g = R_{\text{pump}}(\Delta\nu)\,\big(\Delta n_g^{\text{max}} - \Delta n_g\big) - \gamma_{\text{tr}}\,\Delta n_g = 0$$
  Solving for $\Delta n_g(\Delta\nu)$ and substituting $R_{\text{pump}}(\Delta\nu)$ gives:
  $$\Delta n_g(\Delta\nu) = \Delta n_g^{\text{max}}\,\frac{R_{\text{pump}}(\Delta\nu)}{\gamma_{\text{tr}} + R_{\text{pump}}(\Delta\nu)} = \Delta n_g^{\text{max}}\,\frac{S_{\text{opt}}}{1 + S_{\text{opt}} + 4(\Delta\nu / \Gamma_0)^2}$$
  where $S_{\text{opt}} \equiv \frac{R_0}{\gamma_{\text{tr}}} = R_0\,\tau_{\text{tr}}$ is the **total number of photons scattered during the transit time $\tau_{\text{tr}}$**.
* **Step 3 (Read off the FWHM of $\Delta n_g(\Delta\nu)$):**
  Divide numerator and denominator by $1 + S_{\text{opt}}$:
  $$\Delta n_g(\Delta\nu) = \left(\frac{S_{\text{opt}}\,\Delta n_g^{\text{max}}}{1 + S_{\text{opt}}}\right) \frac{1}{1 + 4\left(\frac{\Delta\nu}{\Gamma_0\sqrt{1 + R_0\tau_{\text{tr}}}}\right)^2} \implies \text{FWHM}_{\text{align}} = \Gamma_0\sqrt{1 + R_0\,\tau_{\text{tr}}}$$
  **Why does a long $\tau_{\text{tr}}$ make $\text{FWHM}_{\text{align}}$ broad?**
  Because $\tau_{\text{tr}} \approx 6\,\mu\text{s}$ is so long ($230\times$ longer than $\tau_{\text{spont}} = 26.2\text{ ns}$), an atom stays in the beam long enough to get optically pumped **even when the laser is detuned far out in the Lorentzian wings** ($\Delta\nu = \pm 10\text{ MHz}$)! At line center ($\Delta\nu = 0$), the alignment hits $100\%$ ceiling early in the transit and saturates (flattens out), while the wings keep growing throughout the $6\,\mu\text{s}$ transit—producing a heavily power-broadened profile of width $\Gamma_0\sqrt{1 + R_0\tau_{\text{tr}}} \gg \Gamma_0$.

### 3. How does subtracting a broad peak (negative Term 2) make a narrow peak sharper?

Write the net signal $y(\Delta\nu)$ as a narrow positive two-level saturation peak $y_1(\Delta\nu)$ ($\text{FWHM}_1 = 6\text{ MHz}$, height $1.0$) minus a broad alignment trough $y_2(\Delta\nu)$ ($\text{FWHM}_2 = 18\text{ MHz}$, depth $-0.30$):
$$y(\Delta\nu) = \underbrace{\frac{1.0}{1 + 4(\Delta\nu / 6)^2}}_{\text{Narrow saturation peak}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{0.30}{1 + 4(\Delta\nu / 18)^2}}_{\text{Broad alignment trough}}$$

Now check the numbers at three points:
1. **At the peak center ($\Delta\nu = 0$):**
   $$y(0) = 1.0 - 0.30 = \mathbf{0.70} \implies \text{The new half-maximum threshold is } \frac{0.70}{2} = \mathbf{0.35}$$
2. **At the old half-maximum detuning ($\Delta\nu = \pm 3.0\text{ MHz}$):**
   At $\Delta\nu = \pm 3\text{ MHz}$, the narrow peak has dropped to half its height ($y_1(\pm 3) = 0.50$), **but the $18\text{ MHz}$-wide negative trough has barely decayed at all**:
   $$y_2(\pm 3) = -\frac{0.30}{1 + 4(3/18)^2} = -0.27$$
   So the net signal at $\Delta\nu = \pm 3\text{ MHz}$ is already down to:
   $$y(\pm 3) = 0.50 - 0.27 = \mathbf{0.23} \quad (\text{well below the } 0.35 \text{ half-maximum!})$$
3. **Where does $y(\Delta\nu)$ actually cross $0.35$?**
   At $\Delta\nu = \pm 2.30\text{ MHz}$, giving a **net FWHM of $4.60\text{ MHz}$ instead of $6.00\text{ MHz}$**!

**Geometric picture:** Over the central $\pm 4\text{ MHz}$ core of the narrow peak, a broad $18\text{ MHz}$ negative peak acts like a downward shift of the peak floor by $\approx -0.27$, while far out in the wings ($\pm 20\text{ MHz}$) both curves return to $0$. Pushing the floor under the central core downward without lowering the outer baseline slices off the bottom $27\%$ of the narrow peak—leaving only the narrower upper tip sticking up above the baseline!

### 4. What about the oscillating electric fields and the spatially varying phase between counter-propagating beams?

#### A. "Don't polarization vectors oscillate back and forth?"
**Yes!** A linearly polarized electric field $\vec{E}(z, t) = E_0 \hat{\epsilon}\cos(kz - \omega t)$ oscillates between $+\hat{\epsilon}$ and $-\hat{\epsilon}$ at the optical frequency $\omega/2\pi = 384\text{ THz}$.
* Therefore, $+\hat{\epsilon}$ and $-\hat{\epsilon}$ describe the **exact same linear polarization axis** (a polarization axis is a headless double arrow $\leftrightarrow$, not a directed vector $\rightarrow$).
* Notice that in our formula, the unit vector $\hat{\epsilon}$ only ever appears squared:
  $$p_\pi = (\hat{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{b})^2 = \cos^2\alpha_\epsilon, \qquad P_2(\cos\alpha_\epsilon) = \frac{3(\hat{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{b})^2 - 1}{2}$$
  Squaring $\hat{\epsilon}\cdot\hat{b}$ makes the physics completely invariant under $\hat{\epsilon} \to -\hat{\epsilon}$ (and corresponds to the cycle-averaged intensity $\langle E^2\cos^2(\omega t)\rangle = \frac{1}{2}E^2$).
* When I referred to "opposite signs of $P_2$ for pump and probe", I meant the **algebraic sign of the quadrupole factor $P_2(\cos\alpha) \in [-1/2, +1]$** (which is $+1$ when a beam's polarization axis is parallel to $\vec{B}$, $\alpha = 0^\circ$, and $-1/2$ when a beam's polarization axis is perpendicular to $\vec{B}$, $\alpha = 90^\circ$)—**not** opposite directions of an oscillating $\vec{E}$ vector!

#### B. "Since the beams are counter-propagating, won't the phase between them vary at every point $z$ along the cell?"
**Yes!** At position $z$ in the cell, the pump has spatial phase $+kz$ and the counter-propagating probe has spatial phase $-kz$. Their relative phase $\Delta\phi(z) = 2kz$ rotates by $360^\circ$ every half-wavelength ($\lambda/2 = 390\text{ nm}$). In $\text{lin}\perp\text{lin}$ ($\hat{\epsilon}_{\text{pump}} = \hat{x}$, $\hat{\epsilon}_{\text{probe}} = \hat{y}$), the total electric field $\vec{E}_{\text{tot}}(z, t)$ forms a spatial polarization grating that cycles through $\text{linear} \to \sigma^+ \to \text{linear} \to \sigma^-$ every $390\text{ nm}$.

**Why doesn't this $390\text{ nm}$ spatial phase grating alter our analysis?** For three independent physical reasons:
1. **Separate pump and probe roles ($I_{\text{probe}} \ll I_{\text{pump}}$):** The strong pump beam ($\hat{x}$) **alone** optically pumps the ground-state populations $n_g(m_B)$, and the intensity of the single traveling pump wave $I_{\text{pump}}$ is uniform in $z$. The weak probe ($\hat{y}$) simply measures the linear absorption coefficient of the medium already prepared by the pump.
2. **Thermal washout along $z$:** Even the atoms that form the "zero-velocity" Lamb dip have a longitudinal velocity spread set by the natural linewidth:
   $$\Delta v_z \sim \frac{\Gamma_0}{k} = \lambda\,\Gamma_0 \approx (780\text{ nm})(6.07\text{ MHz}) \approx 4.7\text{ m/s}$$
   During the $\tau_{\text{tr}} \approx 6\,\mu\text{s}$ transit time required to build up ground-state alignment, these atoms move longitudinally by:
   $$\Delta z = \Delta v_z\,\tau_{\text{tr}} \approx (4.7\text{ m/s})(6\,\mu\text{s}) \approx 28\,\mu\text{m} \approx \mathbf{72\text{ optical wavelengths}}$$
   Flying through $72$ full periods of the $\lambda/2$ grating during a single transit time averages out the relative optical phase $\Delta\phi(z) = 2kz$ for every moving atom.
3. **Macroscopic spatial integration:** Finally, the photodiode measures the integrated absorption across the entire $5\text{--}10\text{ cm}$ cell ($\sim 2 \times 10^5$ half-wavelengths), averaging over all spatial phases $2kz$.
